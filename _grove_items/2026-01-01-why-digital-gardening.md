## Introduction
>A [digital] garden is a collection of evolving ideas that aren’t strictly organised by their publication date. They’re inherently exploratory – notes are linked through contextual associations. They aren’t refined or complete - notes are published as half-finished thoughts that will grow and evolve over time. They’re less rigid, less performative, and less perfect than the personal websites we’re used to seeing.
>
><cite>—Maggie Appleton, “[A Brief History & Ethos of the Digital Garden](https://maggieappleton.com/garden-history)”<cite>

[Maggie Appleton](https://maggieappleton.com) introduced me to the world of digial gardening via a retweet from someone else I was following (if memory serves). My first stab at it used Notion as the platform, which worked pretty well, but when I introduced my friend [Adam](https://www.adamclaxon.com) to the idea he went and coded up his own site from scratch, him being the more keen and skilled developer between the two of us. He was kind enough to share his code base with me, and now here I am using his site as a framework for my own, with far more beautiful results than Notion could offer by a longshot.

Digital gardens are far from common so far, so having given a simple of very accurate idea of what one is (thanks Maggie), and how I jumped on the bandwagon, find here some thoughts about why I think it’s just such a darn good idea, especially against the normal global platform alternatives.

TLDR: It’s human in all the important ways.
## A human scale
Digital gardens today tend to exist as small, independant, personal, hand-built websites (like this one!). It makes it very difficult to amass the hundreds of two-way connections that social platforms encourage, and so the volume of other content I read becomes far less by default. Most ideally, I end up reading a handful of other friends’ gardens on some regular basis, but talking with them about the published thoughts *in person* since it’s normal for gardens to be completely without comments or other ways to respond on the site itself.

Many of the woes of social platforms are a result of their scale. You encounter information and ideas with volumes and speed that nobody is equipped to actually process. If we want to restore humane ways of communicating while holding on to some of the good that today’s capabilities afford, a human scale is one important dimension that digital gardening answers.
## A human pace
I’ve worked in user experience design (UX) and the like for over a decade now, and one error I believe UX has made is to promote too much ease of use to the point that we’ve lost some *helpful* friction in our experiences with tech. Gardening assumes a long process. Whenever an entry is published, it’s likely to be in a “budding” phase that tells you “This is just a first pass, I’m still thinking about this.” We need time to work through ideas, we need time for our ideas to form, change, and mature. We need a lifetime for that process, and even then there is no full arriving at the truth of everything by ourselves. The fast pace of social posting and publishing includes a hurry to be right. Gardening says “let it grow.”
## A human design
Geocities and My Space gave my generation online places to make their own. It was just custom html styling, but it made creating a little nook for ourselves something fun and personal, and the small scale kept it low-stakes. Gardening brings that back since it all lives in very customized personal sites. (I will admit that right now it’s so custom that the bar is *too* high, and I hope that will change in the near future.) The visual choices I make here reflect my personal philosophy, intent, mood, ideals, etc. as much or sometimes more than the words I write.

## How to begin
In the future, I hope I can help others with starting a custom garden themselves. For now, if you’re interested in it, I’d suggest starting with [Notion](https://www.notion.com) or [Obsidian](https://obsidian.md) and publishing your thoughts to the web through their platforms.
