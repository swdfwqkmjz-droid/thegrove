## First thoughts
First off, obviously it’s an amazing device. Apple got the details right, and it clearly makes all the difference for the pleasure of using Vision Pro. The big one for me is its continuity with everything that’s come before. The number of things that look truly new—as opposed to recontextualized—is sparse. They’ve truly been building everything for years with the understanding that it would eventually end up in AR space. If you’ve been using an Apple device for a while, you already know how everything is organized. It's truly high-res where every other device has just been “workable,” it (purportedly) takes up your whole range of vision, and already interacts with the things you wish it would.

But…

![Gif of woman wearing Apple Vision Pro, rotating to reveal the virtual eyes on the outer glass](/assets/images/grove/Apple-WWCD23-Vision-Pro-rotation-230605.gif)
## Second thoughts
The entire vernacular of the interaction mode leans unsteadily on some unspoken assumptions. “We considered how others interact with you very carefully [need actual quote]…” so they present your eyes through the glass, and your entire face to people on a FaceTime call to make your presence felt to others around you. Good ideas, and well executed… except that everyone knows it’s not your real eyes or face. They’ve both been scanned and reproduced. They look quite real, but it’s the knowledge that they’re not that introduces a problem. Meta has been dealing with this issue in the way they speak about the metaverse as well: You can represent yourself however you want, but everyone knows there’s a you behind the presentation that is different and real.

Virtual representations have always had this dimension to them. Someone speaking positively (say, somebody trying to forward the virtues of a metaverse) might treat them as a 1:1: simply another—just as real—expression of your self in a different kind of space. If we take reality to be made up entirely of what our senses tell us, then that can be taken as true. It’s simply a matter of how well we replicate what our senses are used to, not whether it’s real. You could go even further and say that if reality is only what our senses tell us, then anything can be called real even if it doesn’t read to our senses like we expect. Theoretically, you could get there, but convincing ourselves experientially is another matter, and for a very good reason: reality is real on its own, and our sensual experience of it is in fact an experience of more than sense input, rather it’s encountering a something that purports to be what it really is via our senses. Virtual/digital “somethings” purport to be what they are not.

This is a philosophical, epistemological, and ultimately theological point. How can we know anything to be able to call something real or not to begin with? What’s the grounding? If we grant that YHWH is the ground, and has created everything with intention, and given us the ability to accurately sense creation, then that gives us the pulleys to say things like “Those eyes on the screen are fake.” Our souls already intuit this, and so we are unconvinced, even while being amazed.

Another issue is that even if Vision Pro is successful in its aim to connect the user with the people around them, it does not connect them in what the user is actually doing. They remain isolated in their digital view and in whatever it is they are doing. Computer devices have been going this direction for a long time. In the late 90s when my family purchased our first computer, it came with Windows 95, where user profiles were an option you had to manually enable. By Windows 98, those profiles began to take on personality with themes such as aquarium (the best one, obviously). Now, it’s normal for the entire device to be for one person, and if there are profiles, they transform the device into a customized space “with you at the center.”

Vision Pro takes this another leap further by hiding your view from others entirely. On a computer, tablet, or phone, the view was meant for only you, but it was at least exposed to others view as an inherent part of the form. These screens are for your eyes only, as form proceeding from function. At best, it furthers the trend of isolation in digital tech, at worst is turbocharges it. You can hide that show you like with bad language from your kids, or an adulterous chat from your spouse while they sit next to you, looking into your “eyes.” These were both possible before of course, but where ease increases so does action. There’s no chance to set it down with something accidentally left open for anyone to see like before.
Honestly, [Nilay Patel at The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/2023/6/5/23750003/apple-vision-pro-hands-on-the-best-headset-demo-ever) nailed what I’m feeling here:
>I do know that wearing this thing felt oddly lonely. How do you watch a movie with other people in a Vision Pro? What if you want to collaborate with people in the room with you and people on FaceTime? What does it mean that Apple wants you to wear a headset at your child’s birthday party?

## Elevensies
What then do we do with any representation removed from our direct presence? I am typing on a bluetooth keyboard, which sends my button presses to a laptop, which represents them in a browser, which sends the data over the internet to a server where it is sent to your device. I could argue that the degrees between me and you are far more in this case than with a Vision Pro. By degrees, it might be more akin to a hand-written note.
