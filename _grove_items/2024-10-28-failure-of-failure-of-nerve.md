<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p>My experience of Edwin Friedman’s “A Failure of Nerve” is that it’s a self-reinforcing closed loop framework that paints hard-headed leaders as hero martyrs who should never back down from their vision especially when they encounter resistance. It’s been adopted in reformed Christian circles and accepted as compatible with Christian virtues, and is the source of the myth that empathy is a sin.</p>
  <p>It is, however, also a fuzzy matter. There are helpful ideas in the material, and it can appear that the toxicity of the principals depends greatly on the situation they are applied to. How can the material be navigated and evaluated with clarity?</p>
</div>

## Introduction

The first 10 years or so of the millennium contained many trending themes in the church. There were common refrains like “creation fall, redemption, glory.” or others [fill in the blank here]. Among them was a focus on the power of story. It showed up in church circles—especially among the young, restless and reformed. It showed up in business through lifestyle brands and brand stories. Everybody knew that story had a strong effect It could even have a stronger effect than facts. And yet, intellectual, reformed, young, restless, et cetera, types learned to lean heavily on “the facts,” at least in the way that they speak. Into this environmental story came a book called [A Failure of Nerve](https://prod.churchpublishing.org/failureofnerve). The facts of this book are difficult to parse. However, the facts of the book are not the part with the most power. It is its story. And that story has shaped the minds and attitudes of many.

## My journey with A Failure

- First read in 2013 on the recommendation of my former pastor as part of our worship team reading group.
- It seemed helpful in the first read. It gave me an approach to navigate a tense and complicated family situation that seemed effective. 
- It became an intentional part of how the worship team leaders interacted with each other.
- There were statements I questioned and felt uncomfortable with from the beginning, but I found the explanations satisfactory enough alongside my general posture to accept what my pastor said was good material. Generally, the thought was that it needed the gospel to mitigate the potential to use it for selfish or controlling ends, or using it with harshness.
- My attempts to apply Friedman’s principals in my marriage found me becoming cold, stoic, and standoffish toward my wife, which hurt her on numerous occasions.
- I noticed myself becoming cold, stoic, and standoffish toward *anyone* to the degree I applied Friedman to that relationship.
- In the worship leadership team, we noticed some of these tendencies in our interactions, but chalked it up to a misapplication of the ideas rather than a problem with the ideas themselves.
- I became increasingly uncomfortable with how I saw myself and my friends changing the longer we sat under Friedman’s influence. It seemed to me that the gospel was being filtered through Friedman rather than the other way around. While the language we typically used was about being “non-anxious,” we were in fact emotionally unmoved and non-responsive to others’ concerns and needs.
- I noticed overlap in the attitudes that stemmed from Failure of Nerve and the attitudes of political hard-right.
- The discomfort and encounters with my own and others’ coldness continued to the point that I no longer thought of Friedman as helpful or could think of recommending the book. I was having trouble speaking about it with others though because I found it very difficult to pin down how the material lead to these attitudes I saw. It was also difficult because the nature of Friedman’s principals meant my critiques could be easily seen as anxiousness or a lack of maturity since I couldn’t describe the problem in purely objective logical terms.

## Questionable ideas

>I believe there exists throughout America today a rampant sabotaging of leaders who try to stand tall amid the raging anxiety storms of our time.
>
>—p. 2

>It has been my impression that at any gathering, whether it be public or private, those who are quickest to inject words like sensitivity, empathy, consensus, trust, confidentiality, and togetherness into their arguments have perverted these humanitarian words into power tools to get others to adapt to them.
>
>—p. 78

>Increasing one's pain threshold for others helps them mature.
>...trauma is in the experience and the response to it, not in the event itself.
>
>—p. 214

>Differentiation in a leader will inevitably trigger sabotage from the least well-differentiated others in the system.
>
>—p. 215

>The focus on “need fulfillment” that so often accompanies an emphasis on empathy leaves out the possibility that what another may really “need” (in order to become more responsible) is not to have their needs fulfilled.
>
>—p. 146

>Anyone who wishes to advance our species or an institution must possess those qualities which those who have little sense of self will perceive as narcissistic. All this besides the fact that “arrogant,” “headstrong,” “narcissistic,” and “cold” will be the terms used against any person who tries to be more himself or herself.
>
>—p. 190

Imagine applying this to a scenario where you are a leader, and someone comes to you worried about how the church is responding to, oh, I don't know, maybe a global pandemic (theoretically). Perhaps they're noticing that there's a low concern for masking or distancing, etc., and they're really upset because they're concerned about safety, and the church's witness of showing love to others. "Can't you empathize with how scared people are? Can't you empathize with people who are especially at risk?"

Friedman provides a narrative about the situation (or any number of others), and this person or others like them (each of these matches to the quotes above):

- *This situation is not really a big deal, and those who think it is are really just anxiety-ridden. I am standing tall in this storm, and those who are against me are trying to sabotage me.*
- *This anxious person is calling for empathy and togetherness—they're trying to manipulate and strong arm me into doing what they want!*
- *I must remain unmoved by their pain so they can mature.*
- *They think the problem is what's happening. The truth is that the problem is their reaction to it.*
- *The fact that they're trying to sabotage me is proof that I'm healthy and differentiated while they're clearly not.*
- *They think they know what they need but, as a leader, I know what they really need is to not get what they want.*
- *If they call me cold, etc. for not being concerned, then that's proof positive they're an anxious, undifferentiated, immature saboteur. Not only do I not have to listen, it's imperative that I do not listen, and instead hold my ground.*

### Questionable themes

Leaders are always portrayed positively, while anyone who opposes leaders are described as weak, dependent, regressive, recalcitrant, passive-aggressive, anxious, etc.

Part of the overall point of the book is that, in decision making and leadership, the content of a decision, vision, actions, is less important than the ability to take action even if it’s wrong. This sets up a default where the content becomes *entir*e*ly* unimportant and a leader can reassure themselves they are doing well simply because they charge forward and refuse to be held back.

There’s an assumption built in about what makes a “good human.” That they are fully controlled *only* by their rational parts, and never affected at all by external input except by their choice to respond to it.

## Helpful ideas

> Playfulness can get you out of a rut more successfully than seriousness.
—p. ix

> In the search for the solution to any problem, questions are always more important than answers because the way one frames the question, or the problem, already predetermines the range of answers one can conceive in response.
—p. 37

## Differing presuppositions appear to produce wildly different applications

An important question in applying Friedman’s principals is “what counts as an anxious or reactive response?” Is it completely a matter of the person’s affect (E.G. if they’re not calm)? Is it the content of the complaint?

In the space of people like Joe Rigney, anxiety is seen as [belonging to women](https://writing.danielletreweek.com/p/on-the-sin-of-empathy-being-a-woman) and the left. The application, then, is something similar to the example I gave of a church leader downplaying concerns about Covid.

On the other end of the spectrum, [people like Mark Sayers and Skye Jethani](https://www.holypost.com/post/episode-527-does-god-want-public-christian-symbols-non-anxious-leadership-with-mark-sayers) see far right panic over the left as clear examples of pervasive anxiety. Their applications come out in ways that are largely the opposite.

What's the underlying difference, and who's being more faithful to what Friedman says? Does either answer change how helpful the book actually is or not?

## The most damning data point
Just two years before I first read the book, [Douglas Wilson](https://dougwilsonsays.com) gave the book a rave review.
