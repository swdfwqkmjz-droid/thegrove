## Introduction
I’ve thought about this—intent vs. actions—a lot because I had to contend with it in myself. I don't think I've rounded off every corner of it, so to speak, but for me I know that making intent ultimate came from 1 Sam. 16:7:
"The LORD does not look at the things people look at. People look at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart."
and also from Jesus' teachings in Matt. 23:
"You are like whitewashed tombs, which look beautiful on the outside but on the inside are full of the bones of the dead and everything unclean."
“…on the outside you appear to people as righteous but on the inside you are full of hypocrisy and wickedness.”
“First clean the inside of the cup and dish, and then the outside also will be clean.”
There's a couple others I could toss in too, like Romans 7.
It’s not unreasonable to look at all that, or hear a bible teacher talk about it, and conclude something like “Ok, so it’s the inside that matters most. God looks at the heart instead of outward actions, so we should too!”
For me, I first had to run up against situations where it just didn’t seem to work, and forced questions that I needed to resolve. “Why doesn’t it seem to make a real difference in how I act when I mean well? Isn’t the outside of the cup supposed to be clean automatically when the inside is clean?”
After having to confront the real effects of my actions at a practical level, and spending some time thinking about these passages and such, there’s been two major changes in how I think of all this.
1. “People look at the outward appearance” is just a statement of fact, not an indictment. We’re not God, and so we cannot see the heart as he does. That says something very good about God, but it’s not meant to tell us to be like him in that regard. So, someone telling you “my intent is good so only judge me on that!” is making a bad faith argument. You can’t know their intent, you can only tell anything by their actions.
2. Bad actions are bad actions regardless of intent. Bad intent corrupts “good” actions, but good intent does not redeem bad actions. Jesus’ words to the Pharisees and teachers were about looking good on the outside, but being corrupted inwardly. It’s notable that he never mentions the scenario of good intent inside with sinful actions outside. Even Paul doesn’t talk about it the way evangelical teaching does in his whole “I do what I don’t want to do” passage in Rom. 7—he still seems to think that the sinful actions matter even when he doesn’t want to do them. That tells me that it’s not really a thing on the whole.

## But Joseph
The most obvious objection is the story of Joseph.
>Fear not for am I in the place of God? As for you, you meant evil against me; God meant it for good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save many people alive. And now fear not. I will nourish you and your little ones." And he comforted them and spoke kindly to their heart.
>
>—The first book of Moses, commonly called Genesis

If Joseph's brothers can have *bad* intent, and the conclusion is "Oh, well God intended good so it's fine." Then how can we not say that good intent should make things okay? Aren't we supposed ot forgive and forget even when the intent is bad, like Joseph did?

However, Joseph does [tests his brothers extensively](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/common-ground-northeast/id1548000026?i=1000739143298&r=1064) to see whether they are still the same or not before he reveals himself and extends mercy.

>“So even though Joseph recognizes them, he decides to begin giving them a series of tests.
>
...he wants to know, are they still the same old brothers who will sell the youngest son if it benefits them?”
>
>[Erik Thien, "Joseph: The Robe of Mercy" - November 30, 2025](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/common-ground-northeast/id1548000026?i=1000739143298)
